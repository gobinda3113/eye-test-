How it works

Six minutes, replicated procedure

The order matches what happens at the counter: calibrate, distance, occlude, read. Do it the same way each time and the numbers mean something.

  1. 01

    Calibrate your screen

    Hold a bank card against the on-screen outline and slide until the sizes match. That turns millimetres into pixels so the letters are the real size.

  2. 02

    Set your distance

    Pick 3 m or 6 m, mark the spot on the floor, and dim the room. Bright screens and short distances flatter your eyes.

  3. 03

    Cover one eye

    Cup — never press — one eye with your palm, then the other. Both eyes together are tested last.

  4. 04

    Read the smallest line

    Type or tap the letters you actually see. Miss twice in a row and the screener stops you there — guessing hides the problem you came to find.

        What the DMV actually checks

        A licence vision screen is short, and it is remarkably consistent across states. You will be asked to read the smallest line you can on a chart at a set distance, once with each eye and once with both, while wearing your normal correction. That is a Snellen test, and its arithmetic has not changed in a century: the letter subtends five arc-minutes per unit of acuity, so a 20/40 letter is twice the height of a 20/20 letter at the same distance. Most US states draw the unrestricted-licence pass line at 20/40, which is what this screener uses as its benchmark.

        States that cannot rely on the Latin alphabet — or that test children and non-readers — use the Tumbling-E instead, where you report which way the open side of the E points. A field of vision check follows: a technician moves a target into your peripheral vision until you signal that you can see it, and the horizontal extent is recorded. Ordinary licences are typically measured against a 130° to 140° horizontal field, roughly 70° to each side. Colour vision is screened for red versus green using Ishihara-style plates, because those are the signals a driver has to separate at speed.

        Commercial licences add a federal layer. Under 49 C.F.R. § 391.41 a CDL applicant needs 20/40 corrected in each eye separately and in both together, 70° of field in each eye and 130° together, and the ability to recognise red, green and amber. Individual performance on a regular licence does not carry over: a driver who comfortably passes 20/40 binocularly can still fail the per-eye requirement.

        How the five biggest states differ

        The pass line travels well, but the details do not. These are the five rows this site has checked against a primary source — the statute or the agency's own published standard.

        • New York — 20/40. The vision requirement sits in N.Y. Veh. & Traf. Law § 502, and a provider can certify it on form MV-619 instead of your reading the chart at a counter. If you searched for the NYS DMV eye test online because you would rather do it at an optometrist's office, that form is the route.
        • Florida — 20/40, then 20/70 with a 130° field. Florida publishes a 130° horizontal field standard alongside its acuity line, so a field check that would never come up in some states is routine here.
        • Illinois — 20/40, then 20/70 with a 140° field. Illinois also stands out for age: from 75, renewal means a road test as well as a vision check, and the cycle tightens.
        • Texas — 20/40 with both eyes together. The standard lives in 37 Tex. Admin. Code § 15.51, and Texas reads it as a binocular requirement, which is friendlier than a per-eye one.
        • California — 20/40, both eyes together. California's requirement is in the Vehicle Code at § 12804.9 and is likewise treated as a binocular standard for the ordinary licence.

        The practical lesson: read your own state's row in the 50-state table below before you assume anything, and check the citation rather than a summary.

        How to get an accurate result at home

        Screen-based testing fails for one boring reason: nobody knows how big your pixels are. A chart displayed at "about the right size" can be 30 per cent off, which moves a 20/40 line close enough to a 20/25 one to make the result meaningless. This screener solves that the way a clinic does — with a physical reference. Hold any bank card against the on-screen outline and slide until they match; a payment card is 85.6 mm wide by ISO/IEC 7810 ID-1, so the app can convert millimetres to pixels and draw a letter at its true size.

        Then set the room up properly. Stand at 10 feet (3 m) — or 6 m if you have the space, which is the Snellen standard — mark the spot so you stand in the same place every time, dim the lights so the screen is the brightest thing in the room, and keep the screen at eye height. Cover one eye with your palm rather than pressing on it, and do not lean forward to rescue a line: the distance is the measurement.

        The checks, one by one

        Distance acuity. The core test. An adaptive ladder walks from 20/200 down to 20/20, one eye at a time, and stops when you miss. Only as many letters as physically fit at their true size are drawn, so a phone shows a shorter line rather than a shrunken one.

        Tumbling-E. Eight trials from 20/70 to 20/20, with the opening pointing in a random direction. Arrow keys work, which is how the test is delivered to people who cannot read the alphabet.

        Colour vision. Fourteen synthetic Ishihara-style plates. Twelve correct is treated as normal; a missed plate tells you which part of the red-green axis to mention to an optometrist.

        Peripheral awareness. Flashes appear at 60° in eight directions, and a clean run escalates to 75°, so a narrow field shows up as a pattern rather than a single missed dot.

        Near vision. A reading card measured at 40 cm, using the same Snellen arithmetic, which is the part of the exam that catches people over 40.

        The extras. Contrast sensitivity, an astigmatism dial and a duochrome refraction screen are not part of a licence check, but they are the reasons a person who "passes" still complains about night driving or print. They point at a direction, never at a prescription.

        Why a calibrated chart beats a picture of a chart

        Many sites show an image of a Snellen chart and ask you to read it. It looks the same, but it measures nothing: the image is a fixed number of pixels, so the visual angle depends entirely on your monitor and how far you sit from it. Two people can read the same JPEG and be told different things about their eyes. A calibrated chart is the only kind that can claim to reproduce a physical test, which is why the card step here is mandatory rather than optional — and why the letters are drawn as vector optotypes on a 5x5 grid instead of set in a font, so the stroke-to-height ratio stays correct at every size.

        What to bring to the counter

        • Your glasses or contacts, even if you only wear them for driving. Uncorrected testing is the single most common reason for a re-test.
        • Any prescription you have, or the name of the optometrist who wrote it, if your state accepts provider certification such as New York's MV-619.
        • Your current licence or ID, proof of identity, and proof of residency if your state requires it for a renewal.
        • Five spare minutes. The vision check itself is quick; the queue is not.

        CDL, seniors and renewals

        Commercial drivers should rehearse the federal set specifically — per-eye acuity, the Tumbling-E for the field requirement, and a colour run — because the pass line is stricter and the tests are run separately. Older drivers face a different wrinkle: several states shorten the renewal cycle with age, and Illinois adds a road test from 75. New York will accept an eye-care provider's certification on form MV-619, which is often easier to arrange than a slot at a busy office. Whatever your age, the useful preparation is the same: know your numbers before you are asked for them.

        Practice tips

        • Do the whole sequence, not one test. Fatigue and unfamiliarity are what make a second attempt worse than the first.
        • Re-calibrate if you change monitor, zoom level or browser window size. A zoomed page is an uncalibrated page.
        • Write your result down. Comparing this month's line with last month's is more informative than either number alone.
        • Test at the same time of day. Tears, screen glare and tiredness all move the answer.

        What this screener is not

        It is not an official DMV or DOT test, not a medical examination, and it cannot diagnose a condition or produce a prescription. Screen-based results depend on your display, your lighting and your distance, and the state summaries are a guide that may be out of date. Treat it as a rehearsal: it tells you roughly where you stand and what to expect, and it tells you when to book a real appointment.
